<div><img alt="" src="https://media.beehiiv.com/cdn-cgi/image/fit=scale-down,format=auto,onerror=redirect,quality=80/uploads/asset/file/7ecee005-f950-4b93-8770-f02a0f8c9291/Newsletter_Banner.png?t=1789557310"></div><p><span>Hey friend,</span></p><p>Welcome to this issue of the Modern Compass newsletter. Each issue I take one topic from everyday life and we dig a layer deeper together to find new perspectives that creates movement. If someone forwarded this to you, please [<a href="https://themoderncompass.io/newsletter/?utm_source=newsletter.themoderncompass.io&amp;utm_medium=newsletter&amp;utm_campaign=finding-direction-from-an-unlikely-place"><span>subscribe here</span></a><span>] so you don’t miss a future issue.</span></p><p>I recently attended a wedding in Santa Rosa, CA with my wife, our first big solo trip since we started the family 6 years ago. We had the opportunity to explore the town a bit and my wife insisted on visiting the Charles Shulz museum, the creator of the Peanuts comic and cartoons. I was never a huge fan of Peanuts outside of the holiday movies, but she had me convinced to check it out.</p><p>Quick detour from topic, but this is some of the best our deeper relationships have to offer, is the ability to influence our world to see and do things we wouldn’t have otherwise. I touched on this in a recent LinkedIn <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/posts/josh-imholte_i-chose-resonance-and-affinity-to-describe-activity-7526259658186100736-RDY0?utm_medium=ios_app&amp;rcm=ACoAABNh8HkBC6Apg7RVpFNksRzWVFYqD8eZ0iA&amp;utm_source=social_share_send&amp;utm_campaign=copy_link">post</a> a little more. </p><p>As we walked through the Charles Schulz museum, I immediately thought, wow this guy found a direction in life and lived it to the fullest in his own way. I immediately wrote what came to mind that evening from the experience. The following isn’t anything I took word for word from the museum, so if you go, don’t expect this to be listed anywhere. It’s more a synthesis of what I saw and adding in a little of my own perspective. </p><ol start="1"><li><p><b>Follow what makes you curious</b>. Curiosity has a pulling effect, passion doesn’t always. What makes you curious when nothing else is happening?</p></li><li><p><b>Play to your strengths</b>. Charles loved hockey and sports for recreation, but drawing was where he grew his career. </p></li><li><p><b>Have a constructive outlet</b> and embrace what makes you different here. When we were at the museum, a video played that when Charles had a bad day, Charlie Brown did too in his drawings. In a way, it was his outlet aside from the drawing itself. His was creative, but yours doesn’t have to be.</p></li><li><p><b>That outlet can become useful for others</b>. Share it with others. If your outlet isn’t creative and maybe it’s working on cars to take another random example, help another in some way even small that uses your outlet. Lighting anothers candle can brighten your own as they say.</p></li><li><p><b>Find ways to pair curiosity and your outlet </b>to have the career, business, and/or life you desire.</p></li><li><p><b>Beleive</b>. Beleive you have something that no one else is doing or can offer. This takes time to build evidence and it won’t happen overnight. You will probably battle the imposter feeling too, but sharing publicly while hard, is a good way to overcome it and helps you iterate.</p></li><li><p><b>Be willing to play the long game. </b>It took Shulz nearly 3 years of pitching to land Peanuts comic strip. There’s a great insight I heard from another Charles, Charles Duhigg. My takeaway from what Duhigg said is <i>that often if you set off to be successful in something, you will be as successful as you want to be. Along the way, you find how much you really enjoy and/or tolerate the other aspects of it, how well you can endure rejection, and that usually tells you if it’s worth the long game for you</i>. I feel Shulz knew this too.</p></li><li><p><b>Share successes and don’t forget where you came from.</b> For Charles this went beyond the cartoon and global success and bringing that back to his community in a way he loved. He and his wife Joyce built a Switzerland themed ice rink, that eventually was dubbed <i>Snoopys Home Ice</i> in rural Santa Rosa at the time. He built it largely because the only ice rink in town had closed down. It became a huge beacon of community for the town that likely would have never existed otherwise. It all stemmed from his roots being raised in Minnesota and he had many fond memories of his dad flooding the yard so it would freeze to play hockey. If you watch some of the Peanuts movies, you’ll find hockey had a strong influence even in the comics or cartoons. </p><div><img alt="" src="https://media.beehiiv.com/cdn-cgi/image/fit=scale-down,format=auto,onerror=redirect,quality=80/uploads/asset/file/b74d0072-b45d-419f-8405-91b5970a45dd/vimbly1.jpg?t=1794827778"></div></li><li><p><b>Never stop reading and/or learning.</b> Charles was an avid reader that gave him so much more range in his comics.</p></li><li><p><b>Enjoy the simple things that fuel the direction you’ve chosen.</b> After the ice rink was built, Charles would run a routine of breakfast at his restaurant in the ice rink called Warm Puppy Cafe. At the peak of his career, he was making $30-$40 million annually, and he had one table always reserved for him. For decades as a multimillionaire, almost daily he would work in his studio on the comic and walk back and forth to the Warm Puppy Cafe for breakfast and lunch. He loved spending time in a place that brought happiness to him and others. Can you imagine someone doing this in 2026? </p></li></ol><div><img alt="" src="https://media.beehiiv.com/cdn-cgi/image/fit=scale-down,format=auto,onerror=redirect,quality=80/uploads/asset/file/835a05c0-4a3b-4abc-abb4-520791c9e7c6/images.jpeg?t=1794828814"></div><p>I highly recommend a visit to the museum if you are ever in the area, and you can find more about Charles’s life at <a href="https://schulzmuseum.org/timeline/?utm_source=newsletter.themoderncompass.io&amp;utm_medium=newsletter&amp;utm_campaign=finding-direction-from-an-unlikely-place#!/2000">https://schulzmuseum.org/timeline/#!/2000</a>.</p><p>Also they have a drawing room and tracing station at the museum and this was my shot at a comic strip. Snoopy saves the day!</p><div><img alt="" src="https://media.beehiiv.com/cdn-cgi/image/fit=scale-down,format=auto,onerror=redirect,quality=80/uploads/asset/file/37c6eb72-0a60-4352-9a4e-dcace4cb2a48/My_comic.jpg?t=1794843492"></div><div><img alt="" src="https://media.beehiiv.com/cdn-cgi/image/fit=scale-down,format=auto,onerror=redirect,quality=80/uploads/asset/file/2b6a6d2a-3182-4915-a4e5-822c1aaac4b6/Look_a_Layer_Deeper.png?t=1794829184"></div><p>So what does this look like for you/us? I think we can take a lot of inspiration from Charles and many other people we admire. I mentioned this in another LinkedIn post recently, but there are also different levels of finding direction and you should spend some time understanding which one you are working through.</p><div><img alt="" src="https://media.beehiiv.com/cdn-cgi/image/fit=scale-down,format=auto,onerror=redirect,quality=80/uploads/asset/file/c31d2b11-5a8a-4c8d-9468-ac66f930fabe/Finding_Direction__Three_Life_Questions.png?t=1794830266"></div><p>I thought through this a bit deeper on how to simplify gaining direction, and I came to the conclusion of this simple formula. Simple, not easy. :)</p><p><b>What</b>+<b>why</b>+<b>entanglements</b>=<b>Direction</b></p><p>Entanglements are the tough part and where digging deeper is required. You can likely name the what and the why, but the what is deeply entangled with a lot of other things (soft and hard skills, self work, alignment, relationships, etc), ignore that deeper work and you avoid the pursuit or arrive at what you didn’t want.</p><p>When I seriously decided to start writing Modern Compass nearly 2 years ago, I knew what and why (some of it documented in this <a href="https://themoderncompass.io/blog/why-i-wrote-modern-compass?utm_source=newsletter.themoderncompass.io&amp;utm_medium=newsletter&amp;utm_campaign=finding-direction-from-an-unlikely-place">past issue</a>), and this time I understood from some other experiences, I needed to understand the entanglements. Here were just a few:</p><p><b>Low confidence in writing</b> - needed repetition not just writing a book, more at bats writing in public, thus this newsletter started and other avenues as well. </p><p><b>Low trust</b> - When I’ve written in the past, I was terrified of sharing my work partly of being stolen (which was an excuse to not do anything) or that people would think its bad. While I could do more here to improve, bringing in other people is no doubt how you sharpen ideas, I get that now. </p><p><b>Imposter </b>- Why me, why do I think I can speak on these topic with no official background in anything relative to the topics? This one took a long time to get over and it still pops in, but at the end of the day as another author Jay Yang puts it, <i>You can just do things</i>. There’s also the comfort of “<i>nobody cares” </i>philosophy in relation to trying something new. I know it may seem glass half empty, but I don’t see it that way. If something new you try doesn’t land, nobody cares. If it does land, the people who find it useful care and become your audience (or whatever it is you offer or do) and the rest continue not to care. This can actually be quite freeing if you think about it.</p><p><b>How </b>- How will I do this with full time job and 2 young kids. Turns out to Charles Duhigg point I mentioned above, once you get moving you will find out how driven you are to finding a way. This took some time to figure out, but waking up before anyone else is up around 5am most days of the week and using paid time off day here and there is what’s kept things moving forward.</p><p><b>Double down on synthesis</b> <b>skill</b>- Some entanglements are also noticing what you’re good at and sometimes you need to hear it reverberated from others as well. Call it ADHD, call it being observant, or just doing a job for 6 years that required it, but I do feel synthesis, or the break down of information to something simpler and what transpired is a strength of mine. A way to double down on that is to leverage it through writing. </p><p></p><hr><h2 id="activity">Activity </h2><p>Lets assume for a minute you’re just starting out and don’t already have a defined direction you want to go and even if you do, this may help. Take a few minutes to try these in order:</p><ol start="1"><li><p>Sit for 5 minutes or more with your phone off, no distractions and leave the to do list out of your head for a second. In those minutes, what does your mind naturally wonder to? There might just be curiosity lurking there. Extend the time to longer and if it still doesn’t come, maybe create a routing of nothing for 5-10 minutes daily until you do.</p></li><li><p>Derive a high level what and why if you were to pursue something you discovered from step 1 further. Now the hard part, what are all the entanglements that are involved with doing the <i>what </i>well? Self work, trust, relationship building, discipline, and consistency? What about ripple effects from this pursuit, how will you execute, go down the rabbit hole even temporarily. It’s not to think through everything, but knowing your gaps is half the battle of finding direction. </p></li><li><p>Think about trends or patterns where you’ve excelled, this may be hard to yourself and could be worth asking another where they think you excel and bring unique perspective. </p></li></ol><p>If you’ve done the above three things, you may have just discovered a constructive outlet. Do something small today that is a movement into this direction and see how it feels. </p><p></p><hr><h2 id="interactive-activity">Sharing is Caring</h2><p>If you have a great story to share that relates to finding direction or maybe even where you’re actively stuck, I want to hear from you.
